# Which famous creatures are actually safe to use

Companion to `ideas/merge-collector.md`. Short answer: **yes, lots** — most of world
folklore is free, permanently, in every country. But the line isn't drawn where people
usually assume.

Not legal advice; it's the working knowledge you need to design without walking into
something. Get a lawyer's eyes on it before you spend money on art.

---

## 1. The distinction that actually matters

**Copyright protects a specific expression, never the underlying creature.**

- "Dragon" is free. Smaug is not.
- "Vampire" is free — Stoker's *Dracula* (1897) is public domain. Universal's 1931 Bela
  Lugosi look, with the widow's peak and the high collar, is a protected depiction.
- "Nine-tailed fox" is free; it's a thousand-year-old Japanese folk figure. Ninetales, the
  specific Pokémon design, is not.
- "Giant ape" is free. King Kong's specific design and name are contested property.

So the question is never *may I use a phoenix* (always yes) but *does my phoenix look like
someone's phoenix*. Two rules follow from that:

1. **Work clean-room.** Don't have Pokémon or Digimon reference open while drawing. Not
   because a court will subpoena your browser history, but because you'll unconsciously
   converge, and "substantially similar" is judged on the result.
2. **Design first, name second.** A creature that looks original with a folkloric name is
   safe. A creature that looks like Charizard is a problem no matter what you call it.

## 2. Deep, genuinely safe sources

### World folklore and mythology — the big one

Nobody owns these. They predate copyright by centuries and no one can claw them back.

| Region | Creatures |
|---|---|
| Greek / Roman | Phoenix, griffin, chimera, hydra, cerberus, harpy, satyr, minotaur, siren |
| Norse | Kraken, Fenrir, Jörmungandr, huldra, draugr, nisse |
| Japanese yōkai | Kitsune, tengu, kappa, tanuki, oni, baku, nurarihyon, karakasa |
| Chinese | Qilin, pixiu, long (dragon), nian, jiangshi, huli jing |
| Slavic | Domovoi, rusalka, leshy, vodyanoy, zmey |
| Celtic / British | Selkie, kelpie, banshee, púca, will-o'-the-wisp, redcap |
| Middle Eastern / Persian | Roc, simurgh, djinn, manticore, karkadann |
| Filipino | Tikbalang, aswang, sarimanok |
| Latin American | Chupacabra, cadejo, alux |
| Modern cryptid | Yeti, Bigfoot, Mothman, Nessie, jackalope, Jersey Devil |

This is a bottomless well, and the less-mined corners — yōkai beyond the famous four, Slavic
house spirits, Filipino folklore — give you distinctiveness for free.

### Real animals, living and extinct

Completely unrestricted. Nobody owns the axolotl, the pangolin, the mantis shrimp, the
tardigrade, *Anomalocaris*, or any dinosaur. A merge chain running from a real animal up
into its mythic counterpart is a strong structure and costs you nothing legally.

### Pre-1930 literature

In the US, published works from **1930 and earlier** are public domain as of 2026 (95-year
term). In the UK and EU it's life of the author plus 70 years, so authors who died before
1956. Both tests must pass if you're shipping worldwide.

Safe by this route: Lovecraft's creatures including Cthulhu (published 1928, and he died in
1937), Carroll's Jabberwock, Shelley's creature, Homer, Ovid, the Brothers Grimm, *One
Thousand and One Nights*, Dante's demons.

### Explicitly licensed material

- **D&D SRD 5.1 / 5.2** — Wizards released these under **CC BY 4.0** in 2023/2024. Genuinely
  usable commercially with attribution. Note it deliberately *excludes* their Product Identity
  monsters (below).
- CC0 and CC BY asset libraries — fine, just track attribution obligations per asset.

## 3. The traps — things that look free and aren't

| Looks like folklore | Actually owned |
|---|---|
| Beholder, mind flayer / illithid, displacer beast, githyanki, umber hulk, yuan-ti, slaad | Wizards of the Coast Product Identity — excluded from the CC-licensed SRD on purpose |
| Hobbit, balrog, ent, Nazgûl, mithril | Middle-earth Enterprises; "Hobbit" is trademarked and enforced |
| Tyranid, genestealer, ork (that spelling) | Games Workshop, litigious |
| Godzilla, Mothra, Rodan | Toho, extremely litigious. "Kaiju" as a generic word is fine |
| Slender Man | Authored 2009 by Eric Knudsen — modern creepypasta, not folklore |
| SCP Foundation entries | CC BY-SA — usable, but share-alike is a real burden on a commercial game |
| Krampus, Baba Yaga *specific film versions* | The folk figures are free; recent designs aren't |

Note the pattern: **generic fantasy races are fine** (elf, dwarf, orc, goblin, troll, giant —
all folkloric, all predating Tolkien), while a specific invented species from a specific
franchise is not.

---

## 4. One non-legal caution

Legally free isn't the same as consequence-free. Sacred figures from living traditions —
Indigenous North American, Māori, Aboriginal Australian, and religious figures generally —
are unprotected by copyright but can generate real backlash if used carelessly as
collectible monsters. The Thunderbird and the Wendigo are the two most commonly misused;
the Wendigo in particular carries specific meaning in Algonquian cultures.

Practical version: prefer folklore from traditions where the figures are already treated as
entertainment in their own culture (Japanese yōkai and European monsters both qualify — Japan
has been putting yōkai in comedies for centuries), and skip anything that's currently sacred
to someone.

## 5. The design argument against leaning on famous creatures

Here's the thing worth thinking about, separate from the law.

The pleasure of a creature collector is **discovery**. You're chasing something you haven't
seen. If every creature is one you already know — here's a griffin, here's a minotaur — you've
built an encyclopedia, not a collection. Familiarity is exactly the wrong feeling at the point
of reveal.

But recognition is a genuinely powerful payoff *if you spend it at the right moment*.

**So: original creatures in the low tiers, famous folklore at the apex.** Tiers 1–6 are your
own designs — small, odd, unfamiliar, discoverable. Tiers 7–10 resolve into something the
player suddenly recognises. The moment a chain of strange little embers you've been merging
for a week finally becomes an unmistakable **phoenix** is worth more than eighty familiar
creatures, and it makes the top of every chain feel like an arrival.

It also solves your art problem's hardest part: apex forms are the ones that need to be
spectacular, and folklore gives you centuries of visual reference to work from.

## 6. A concrete set for Hatchery — real animals that became myths

The zoo/safari instinct and the mythology instinct resolve into one thing, and it's better
than either alone.

**Every chain starts as a real animal and climbs into the myth that real animal produced.**
Not an arbitrary pairing — these are the actual historical derivations. People saw these
animals and invented these creatures from them.

| Family | Tier 1–4 (real) | Tier 8–10 (myth) | The real historical link |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ember | Salamander | **Phoenix** | Medieval Europe believed salamanders lived in fire |
| Tide | Giant squid | **Kraken** | Norse sailors' squid encounters |
| Hollow | Fox | **Kitsune** | Direct — the fox *is* the kitsune |
| Stone | Hippopotamus | **Behemoth** | Behemoth has long been identified with the hippo |
| Gale | Eagle | **Roc** | Travellers' tales of oversized eagles |
| Rime | Brown bear | **Yeti** | The Himalayan brown bear is the leading explanation |
| Verdant | Giraffe | **Qilin** | Zheng He's 1414 giraffe was presented at court as a qilin; Japanese still calls the giraffe *kirin* |
| Umbra | Tapir | **Baku** | 獏 is the Japanese word for both the tapir and the dream-eater |

Why this is the right answer:

- **It keeps evolution-as-merge intact.** A straight zoo game can't do this — merging two
  zebras into a giraffe is nonsense, and you'd be back to arbitrary chains, which is the
  exact problem the evolution idea solved. Animal → myth is a chain with a real direction.
- **It gets the warmth of real animals** at the bottom, where players spend most of their
  time, plus reference photos for the tiers you'll draw most often.
- **It earns the apex payoff.** Recognition lands hardest at tier 10, exactly where you want
  it, and tiers 5–7 are yours to make strange as the animal drifts toward the myth.
- **It's a marketing line and an art bible in one sentence:** every creature here is a real
  animal and the legend people built out of it. Nobody else in the merge category is saying
  anything that specific.

All eight myths are public domain worldwide, none belongs to a company, and none is sacred
to a living tradition in a way that would cause trouble. Draw them your own way and you're
clear.

## 7. Naming — don't call it Merge Anything

"Merge Safari" and "Merge Zoo" are both taken several times over, and the animal-merge
namespace generally is saturated: [Merge Safari – Fantastic
Isle](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.SuperAwesome.MergeSafari), [Merge
Zoo by Game Circus](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.gamecircus.zoo), [Zoo
Merge](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.smashlabjoko.zoomerge), [Zoo Merge:
Match Game](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.rnstudio.animalmerge), [Merge
Animals – My Perfect Zoo](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.westover.animals),
and more. Note that "Merge Animals Zoo: Safari Park" ships under the package id
`com.noxgames.merge.puppet.soccer` — a reskinned soccer game. That's the quality floor of
this category, which is both a warning and an opportunity.

Three reasons to avoid a `Merge <Noun>` title:

1. **No trademark.** A purely descriptive title can't be protected, so you can never stop
   anyone cloning your name.
2. **Hopeless ASO.** You'd be competing for "merge zoo" against established games with
   millions of installs. A new developer ranks nowhere on those terms, ever.
3. **It signals shovelware.** The category's reputation is set by reskins, and a generic
   title puts you in that bucket before anyone sees a screenshot.

**Do what the category's actual winners do:** a distinctive brand name, with the keyword in
the subtitle. Travel Town — Merge Adventure. Gossip Harbor: Merge & Story. Merge Mansion is
the exception that proves it, and it launched with a large marketing budget.

Suggested: **Wildlore — Merge & Evolve.** One word that carries both halves of the concept
(real wildlife + folklore), distinctive enough to be protectable, and it appears unused on
both stores as of August 2026. Other directions: Beastlore, Menagerie, The Wilding, Kirin.

Search any candidate on USPTO TESS, UKIPO and EUIPO in Class 9 and Class 41 before buying a
domain — a store search only tells you about apps, not about trademark holders in other
industries who can still object.

## 7. Before you ship

- **The app name is the real trademark risk**, far more than any creature name. Search it on
  USPTO TESS, the UKIPO and EUIPO registers in Class 9 (software) and Class 41
  (entertainment) before you commit to it or buy a domain.
- Keep your sketches and iterations. Independent creation is a defence, and dated work files
  are how you show it.
- Don't use the word "evolution" alongside anything else that reads Pokémon-adjacent — the
  mechanic is free, but a stack of small resemblances is what makes a case.
- Avoid throw-a-ball-to-capture entirely. That's the mechanic Nintendo asserted patents over
  against Palworld in 2024, and merging keeps you well outside it anyway.
